Starting point · The problem

The Mars data exists. What is missing is bringing it together at the scale of one EVA.

Tools such as Mars Trek and the USGS-NASA geologic mapping were built to look at Mars by region. A walk fits within a few kilometers.

At that scale, someone in a suit needs direct answers: where to step, how far is left, whether the radio reaches, and whether the oxygen covers the way back.

1

Stop 1 · Two screens

Plan in the habitat. Follow it outside.

In the habitat

Mission tablet

  • 3D map with the real terrain and imagery of Gale crater.
  • Each click on the map becomes a stop. The route leaves the habitat, visits them all and returns by the safest path.
  • The time control moves the Sun, redraws the shadows and tells how much daylight is left.
  • The safety panel shows oxygen, radio, sand and shelter before anyone goes out.

During the EVA

Helmet visor

  • The view of the terrain stays clear. Always on screen, only the essentials: heading, next stop, oxygen, battery and walk-back.
  • A guide on the ground shows the way to the next stop.
  • Alerts appear by severity, and the emergency protocol is walked through step by step.
  • Everything else opens on request, by touch or by voice.
2

Stop 2 · Layered data

Six kinds of data over the same ground.

The challenge asks for at least three. Each layer has its mission and instrument on record.

  1. OpticalHiRISE, MRO orbiterPhotos from 25 cm to 2.5 m per pixel
  2. Topographic1 m elevation model, USGSTerrain, slope and routes
  3. ThermalTHEMIS, Mars Odyssey orbiterNight thermal image
  4. AtmosphericREMS, Curiosity roverTemperature and pressure
  5. RadiationRAD, Curiosity roverDose over the last 30 days
  6. GeologicUSGS-NASA geologic mapGeologic unit of the area

On top of them the prototype calculates slope, walk-back, radio reach and soft sand. The sand is a team estimate, from the color of the image.

3

Stop 3 · Safety first

Before the science, the way back.

The safety panel answers, on the real terrain, the questions that decide the EVA.

  • Can I walk back?

    The oxygen has to cover the walk from any point to the habitat, at 2 km/h, with one hour in reserve.

    Calculated

  • Does the radio reach?

    A straight line links the suit antenna to the habitat. If the terrain is in the way, there is no direct signal.

    Calculated

  • Where do I shelter?

    The farthest point of the route already comes with the nearest terrain shelter marked.

    Calculated

  • How much radiation?

    The dose comes from Curiosity's RAD instrument, averaged over the last 30 days.

    Measured

  • Will it get cold?

    The tablet warns in which part of the EVA the air drops below the suit minimum. The visor warns one hour ahead.

    Assumption

  • What if it goes wrong?

    Dust storm, solar event, loss of radio, vehicle failure: each scenario has a protocol.

    Assumption

81%of the sample traverse has no line of sight to the habitat. The panel shows it before anyone goes out.

4

Stop 4 · Onboard AI

An AI that only quotes numbers that came from the data.

On the tablet and in the visor, the conversation is with a language model playing the onboard computer. With every question it receives the EVA data from that instant.

The answer is checked before it appears, and a number with no origin does not get through. Alerts and protocols follow fixed rules and do not depend on it.

It speaks and listens in English and Portuguese, with two open models: Kokoro for speech and Whisper for listening.

Onboard AI

  1. Is there enough daylight leaving at this time?

    Yes. The EVA is expected to end at 16:33, 1h26 before sunset at 17:56.

  2. Where does the route lose radio?

    The route has 7.3 km without direct radio to the habitat, across 6 stretches; the current data does not identify their exact locations.

  3. Does the oxygen cover the walk-back from anywhere on the route?

    Yes—the plan says the suit oxygen covers a walk-back to the habitat from any point on the route. This is calculated, not measured; the prototype has no real suit.

Real answers from the onboard AI about the sample traverse, recorded on November 15, 2026. The emphasis was added on this page.

5

Stop 5 · Where each piece of data comes from

Every piece of Mars data has a source.

Nothing was invented. What is a calculation, an assumption or a simulation is labelled on screen.

Measured
Measured by an orbiter or a rover: terrain, imagery, weather and radiation.
Calculated
A calculation on real data: routes, slope, radio, Sun position and shadows.
Assumption
A team choice: vehicle speed, alert thresholds, suit battery and protocols.
Simulated
The astronaut's position in the visor and the failures in the training scenarios.

The full list, with the link for each item, is at the end of the tablet panel, in the demo.

6

Stop 6 · How it was built and where it stops

It runs in the browser. It has limits, and they are in plain sight.

Built with

  • Plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript, with no build step.
  • Three.js for the terrain, the globe and the visor in 3D.
  • Least-cost routes (A*) over the terrain, with Tobler's hiking function.
  • Sun position from NASA's Mars24 algorithm.

Limits

  • The terrain uses an 8-meter grid. Smaller rocks do not show up.
  • Soft sand is an estimate from the color of the image.
  • There is no GPS on Mars. The astronaut's position is simulated.
  • The emergency protocols are a team proposal.
  • The AI and the live measurements need a network connection.
